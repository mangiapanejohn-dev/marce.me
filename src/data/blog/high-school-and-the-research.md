High school started. Ordinary Chinese curriculum, gaokao track, class of 2029.

It began in the same month that the work I care about stopped being a hobby. Three weeks in August: an event I was responsible for that several hundred people actually attended, a security incident that made me rebuild how an entire application authorizes writes, and the point where an agent-runtime project went from _thing I tinker with_ to the first problem I've had that I can imagine still working on in five years.

Those two things — the schedule and the work — arrived at the same time and they do not fit together on their own.

### What I'm actually asking for

I want to be precise here, because there's a story that would be easier to tell and it's the wrong one.

I'm not dropping out. I'm not asking to skip subjects because I find them boring. I'm not claiming that what I do at a laptop is more valuable than mathematics. The coursework gets done and the exams get sat, and I don't think that's a compromise I'm making — I think a lot of what I need in order to be good at this is on that syllabus, and I'd be an idiot to trade it away for more hours of the thing I already enjoy.

What I've been talking to my school about is narrower: whether some portion of the time that is schedulable can go to structured independent technical research, with the coursework and exam requirements met as normal. Not free time. Time with a shape — a stated problem, a written plan, artifacts at the end, something to show that's checkable by someone other than me.

That conversation is in progress. Not granted, not refused, being worked out — which is roughly what I expected, and honestly more openness than I'd assumed I'd get. I'm writing it down now rather than after because I'd rather have a record of what I asked for than a tidy account of how it turned out.

The other thing that's helped is that the work isn't purely mine. The hackathon I'm the CTO for is a place students who actually like building things can go and come back with something real. That's a thing a school can look at and evaluate, which makes the whole conversation less abstract than _let me have some afternoons_.

### Fall 2029

Around the same time I started doing something that felt premature and turned out not to be: asking universities how this works.

Not applying. It's three years out. But the pathway for someone on a standard Chinese curriculum applying abroad is genuinely non-obvious — what a gaokao score means to an admissions office, which subject choices close doors early, what the English requirements look like, how need-based aid actually works for an international student. Those aren't questions you want to be researching in your final year, because several of the answers constrain decisions you make now.

I'm deliberately not listing schools. Some of that is that I don't know yet. Most of it is that a list of prestigious names is a way of talking about yourself while sounding like you're talking about your plans, and I've read enough of those.

### The question I'd rather answer

The framing I keep being handed is: what should I do to get in?

The usual answer is to accumulate. Ten activities, a competition here, a volunteer thing there, a leadership title, breadth. I understand why the incentive exists and I don't think people who follow it are being foolish. I just don't think it's true, and more importantly it's not what I want the next three years to have been.

The question I'd rather answer is: **if I have three years, what is actually worth becoming good at?**

I have a partial answer, and it's not a list of achievements. It's a way of working:

- Build things that are hard enough that they break.
- When they break, find out why — actually why, down to the mechanism, not to the point where a plausible story appears.
- Learn the theory required to understand the failure. In practice this has meant distributed systems, database authorization models, protocol design, and a lot of stuff about state and lifecycle I didn't know I needed.
- Write it down while it's still true, including the parts where I was wrong.
- Publish what can be published.
- And stay with one problem long enough to understand something about it that isn't obvious.

That last one is the whole bet. Anyone can get to the shallow version of ten fields in three years. Almost nobody stays somewhere long enough to reach the part where the interesting questions are, because the interesting questions look like tedium from the outside — a week arguing with yourself about which component is allowed to close a session does not photograph well.

I have very little evidence that this is the correct strategy for university admissions. I have quite a lot of evidence that it's the correct strategy for becoming someone worth admitting, and I've decided to treat those as the same problem and be wrong about it if I'm wrong.

### Where the three years go

Into the runtime, mostly.

I've spent the last month convinced that the difficult and under-explored part of AI systems is not the model. It's the machinery around it — what owns a task, where state lives, how a system asks a person for permission without losing itself, how you find out afterwards why it did what it did, how it checks its own work. Those questions have not run out on me. Every one I close opens a more fundamental one underneath.

Meanwhile there are exams, and a syllabus, and a normal school year, and I'm going to do those too.

I don't think this is a conflict I resolve. I think it's one I schedule.

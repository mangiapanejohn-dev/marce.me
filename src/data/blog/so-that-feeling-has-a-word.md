Everyone says I'm a block of wood.

This I'll admit.

Someone put it harsher — said I have no tear ducts. Said if the planet actually blew up, I'd probably go "oh" and get back to whatever I was doing. 🤠

Yeah. 😐

Sounds about right.

I usually don't argue. Someone rips into me and I mostly just nod. Sometimes I'll even go along with it — yeah, you're right.

Not because I'm some enlightened guy. It genuinely doesn't get in. I hear it and it's gone, like an unsaved temp file. Close the window, gone.

So by rights I shouldn't be the one writing this.

But, well. At the end of this summer our team spent three days in Aranya. **On the last night a bunch of us sat on the lawn and everyone told their own story, one at a time.**

<img src="/feeling-lawn-night.jpg" alt="The lawn at night — one lit tent, a line of candles down the steps, hills behind it" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 12px; box-shadow: 0 4px 16px rgba(0, 0, 0, 0.1); margin: 2rem 0;" />

I just sat there and listened. Didn't say a word.

And somewhere in the middle of listening, something showed up in me that had never showed up before.

At the time I genuinely couldn't describe it. Not sad. Not about to cry. Uh — okay, not *entirely* not-sad either. It was just strange, like something somewhere paused for a second and you don't know why it paused.

I didn't do anything about it. Mostly because I didn't know what there was to do.

A few days after I got home, I found out what it was called.

I'll come back to that. First I have to explain how I ended up like this.

### The defect started in Minecraft

I was actually pretty outgoing as a kid.

Nobody believes that when I say it, but it's true — I talked a lot, completely different animal from now. Then my dad banned phones and only let me use the computer. I still don't understand the logic. Maybe sitting at a desk with a keyboard in front of you just *looks* more like doing something real.

I did not play any less. I just swapped the phone for a computer.

Minecraft first, obviously. Normal at the start — mining, building houses, running around. Then I hit commands, hit command blocks, and I stopped being normal immediately: you type one line in and the weather changes, time stops, rules that were already decided get overwritten by you.

Damn. 🤠

I was completely gone. **Compared to playing *inside* the world, I wanted to know how the world was running in the first place.**

Then I started messing with servers, including blowing up other people's servers. Looks childish now, and yeah, it crossed a line — there wasn't much in my head at the time beyond: oh, so you can do *that*. Let me try.

Using something the normal way has never done anything for me. I see an interface, I want to know what's behind it. I see a feature, I want to know how it was made. I see a rule, and my first instinct isn't to follow it — it's whether it can be changed, worked around, or whether I could just build my own version.

That's the defect. That's always been the defect.

Then I started learning to code. Python, C++, servers, systems, reverse engineering — whatever I ran into, I learned. I have no idea where the energy came from. Maybe kids genuinely don't need sleep hhh 💀

There was a stretch in there where I got into some grey-area stuff. Basically one question in my head at the time: is it technically possible. Whether it *should* be done, I didn't think about much. Eventually I hit the boundary. Paid for it too.

I'm not going to unpack that part. Write too much about it and it starts reading like a flex, which is dumb.

Anyway, I found out later that ***can* and *should* really are not the same thing.** I didn't know that from the start.

### Code has logs. People don't.

Spend long enough around computers and you change. I did, anyway.

Code is honestly easy to deal with. Wrong is wrong, an error is an error. Follow the logs, follow the data flow — annoying, sure, but you always find the line that's broken in the end.

People aren't like that. **People don't have logs.**

When a person suddenly isn't happy, no window pops up saying:

> Error: that thing you just said was wrong.

It says nothing. You just guess. And your guess might be wrong anyway.

I'm not good at this. Genuinely. It's not that I don't want to talk to people — a lot of the time I just don't know what to say. There's clearly something in my head, and the moment I have to open my mouth it's empty. Like the input box is lit and the cursor's blinking, and nothing comes out.

Oh right, one more thing along the way.

I met Siyan in Chengdu a while back. He ran a college hackathon, so I went. By the actual results, first place should have been mine.

But I'm not a college student.

The judges got stuck right there — said first place couldn't go to me. The person who actually got first said themselves that the prize should've been mine. The people around me were angrier than I was, taking turns being outraged on my behalf.

And my reaction?

Oh. Sure. ☝️

Genuinely nothing. Not fake nonchalance — there was actually nothing there.

Someone else might have felt bad, felt it was unfair, chewed on it for a long time. I don't. At least I didn't then.

### Talking to AI goes way smoother than talking to people

Then AI showed up. That one changed a lot for me.

Because I found out I talk to AI way more smoothly than I talk to people. It knows more, it gets there faster. I say half a thing and it usually picks up the rest. Give it enough background, state the goal clearly, and it just goes — I don't have to guess what it's secretly thinking.

Here's an opinion a lot of people won't like: **I think the knowledge, the reasoning, and that IQ number everyone loves to argue about — for today's top AI, all of it already exceeds any single human being.** Not "exceeds in some areas." The total isn't even the same dimension.

People say: AI makes mistakes too, when it counts you still need a human.

Sure. Of course it makes mistakes. But I don't love the reflex of blaming the model the second something breaks. My first instinct is usually to trace back:

- Did I not state the background clearly
- Did I not give it the full project files
- Was the context itself broken
- Was the goal too vague
- Or should this task never have been handed to a single agent to run end-to-end in the first place

Nine times out of ten, that's where the problem actually is.

And the rest, the ones that really are the model — fine. Write down why it went wrong and how to avoid it next time, turn that into a rule, push it back into the workflow. Done.

So something breaking isn't necessarily bad for me. Fix it once and the system gains one piece of experience — and it's not just me who learned it, it's me, the AI, the knowledge base, the workflow, all of us gaining a little.

What I learn into my own head, I forget. Fast, too. **What gets written into the system doesn't just vanish because I slept.** In theory, anyway.

### So I built myself a vault: Marc's 01 Brain

It isn't a normal notes app.

How do I put this. Normal notes: you put things in, you search them out later. **What this one stores is more like *me*.**

What decisions I made and why I chose that way at the time; which judgment call was wrong and where exactly; what broke in a project and what rule got added afterward; how I talk, how I work, what I care about, and what I keep pretending not to care about. All in there.

On top of it there's another layer called **OMEGA**. I write it as **ø**. No deep meaning, mostly it's short and easy to type.

ø is for retros and for reasoning forward. It doesn't write my diary for me — a diary is more like putting the past somewhere and leaving it there. ø takes the past back out and shoves it directly into the next decision.

One time, after a retro, it told me something I had genuinely never noticed about myself. It said: every time I say "whatever," "either's fine," "doesn't matter" — the behavior right after is actually very specific.

Meaning I already had an answer. Often a very clear one. I just don't say it.

I was a little stunned reading that, because I really hadn't seen it. It didn't guess that from one conversation — it lined up dozens of records side by side, and *then* it showed up.

People forget. People also get dragged around by whatever mood they were in. Sometimes you finish doing something and can't even explain why you did it that way.

Records don't do that.

So I've always felt that **in a certain sense, that vault knows the complete me better than the me of any single moment does.** When I said that, I mostly meant projects and decisions.

Turns out it wasn't only that.

### "You don't have a website?" "Correct."

Then it was 2026. I'm fifteen, with the high school entrance exam coming up.

By normal logic I should have been revising. Clearly not normal.

I came across something called **Horizon** on Xiaohongshu. A group of young people putting together a summer program — sign-ups, teams, projects, and an in-person stretch in Beijing over the break. I wanted to know more, so I found Peter, the founder, and asked him for the website.

He said there wasn't one.

I said you don't have a website? He said correct.

A team about to run an event. No website.

My first reaction wasn't even whether this team was legit. My first reaction was: **I'll make one then.**

There is nothing inspiring about how this started. No "I saw the idealism in these people." No "I decided to join them and change the world." None of that. I just happened to be bored around then, and happened to know how to build websites.

You don't have one. I'll make one.

And then I was their first engineer.

Looking back, a lot of my things start exactly like this: do it first, and whatever it turns into, deal with that later. Very coder of me, honestly.

Everything I'd built before basically lived on my own machine. If it ran, it ran. If it errored, I'd fix it tomorrow. If some corner was rough, whatever — I was the only user, nobody was waiting.

I'd never had that feeling before.

And the team didn't treat me like a kid dropping by for the experience because I'm fifteen. They actually handed me the important things, and when it broke, they actually came to me. Peter wasn't standing next to me every day going "I believe in you." There was no child-prodigy storyline — they were just willing to hand things over, and I was willing to take them.

And most of the time I actually caught them.

Yeah. Most.

### A team that has no idea how to comfort anyone

The people on that team — Peter, Shao Yiming, Echo, Cathy — all of them are ridiculous.

A thing can go from "let's drop it" to "we're doing it right now" inside thirty seconds. Plan decided at noon, thrown out by evening, picked back up the next morning. Nobody explains why it got thrown out yesterday. Nobody explains why it got picked back up today. We just keep going.

The group chat basically never stops. 2 a.m., you figure everyone must finally be asleep, you send one message — instant reply. And then not a single person will admit they're up late.

One night I was fixing something and had lost track of the hour completely. I looked up and there were still three people in the room, everyone doing their own thing, nobody talking.

I looked at them for a second.

Oh.

Then put my head back down and kept working.

<img src="/feeling-allnighter.jpg" alt="Late-night living room, everyone parked behind their own laptop" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 12px; box-shadow: 0 4px 16px rgba(0, 0, 0, 0.1); margin: 2rem 0;" />

The Beijing days were messier. Daytime at the venue, then back to where we were staying to keep going at night. There was always more to do than there was time, and someone would always drop a "this needs to be ready tomorrow." Someone's writing copy, someone's walking the flow, someone's arguing with the venue people, someone fell asleep in a corner and nobody woke him.

We talked about work through meals too. And then the talking turns into making fun of each other, into saying completely unhinged things, like we'd finally stopped working. And then somebody goes:

> Oh crap, that thing isn't done.

And the table empties. Everyone back to their own thing.

When someone's about to crack, everyone else goes over and bothers them.

Yes. Bothers. **This team is not good at comforting people.** Their version of comfort is to keep bugging you, poking you, asking you things, deliberately messing with you, until you don't have time to keep cracking.

I'd never been anywhere like that. What I was used to is: something's broken on my end, I fix it myself. If I can solve it, I solve it. If I can't, that's also my problem.

Those days were a little different. What exactly was different, I didn't think about at the time. No time to think about it.

### The thirty-odd minutes before the deadline

Close to the submission deadline, the submission feature still wasn't finished.

There was a SQL function in it that I'd had AI write earlier, and it had been hiding a bug the whole time. The worst part: **it ran fine, and was wrong the whole time.**

That night I'd planned to just keep going with Claude. I'd just paid $125 for the membership. Money gone. Membership never arrived.

I refresh. Still Free. Refresh again. Still Free.

What? Money gone, no membership. 😭

I really wanted to swear at someone. But there was no time for that, so I limped along with other AI on and off while doing the frontend and the database myself.

That bug was buried deep. Not a syntax error, not some obvious beginner thing — the function had the whole data flow slightly wrong in its head. The parameters coming back from the frontend were correct in themselves, but the positions were wrong. So it ran, it returned results, the results were just wrong.

That kind is the worst. The editor doesn't underline anything red. The logs don't hand you the answer. Every piece looks fine on its own, and together it's just wrong.

I lined up the frontend request, the database fields, the function definition, the returned result, over and over. Thirty-something minutes of that.

What state was I in for those thirty-something minutes? Dying of sleep. Extremely irritated. Fix one thing, two more appear.

There was no calm analysis. No sudden shaft of light where I snap and find the truth. None of that. Just swearing internally and continuing to scroll.

Afterward I did what I always do and wrote this one into the knowledge base. Not as "AI is bad." As: why it misread this project's data flow, what I failed to give it up front, how a task like this should be opened next time, how to split it up and verify it.

I always handle it this way. **Yelling at the model does nothing. Turning the problem into a rule you won't step on again — that does something.**

### The game of pool that never happened

By the time it was fixed I was tired enough to fall asleep where I sat. Echo suddenly said:

> Come on, let's go play pool.

This is just how we are. Someone thinks of something. We go. No discussion in between.

Then we discovered every ride-hailing app was in arrears. All of them. Genuinely all of them.

After a lot of fighting with it we finally got a car. The car pulls up and stops in front of us. We walk over. We take one look at the driver.

And then it suddenly hits us that… never mind. Too tired.

So we turned around and ran. Actually ran. Cancelled the order on the spot. That driver probably still has no idea what was wrong with those kids. 💀

### The last night, on the lawn

<img src="/feeling-stories.jpg" alt="The lawn on the last night — candles in a line, someone holding a guitar, everyone else sitting and listening" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 12px; box-shadow: 0 4px 16px rgba(0, 0, 0, 0.1); margin: 2rem 0;" />

I've forgotten who started it. Anyway, everyone started telling their own story, one at a time. Normally this group is unbearably loud — the second anyone gets serious, everyone else has to make fun of them first. That night nobody cut in.

One person talked, everyone else sat there and listened, and they were saying things nobody would ever normally bring out.

And listening to it, I suddenly realized **everyone here has one of those.** Everyone has something they don't say, and don't know how to say. Normally they all look more unhinged than the next; sit them down and none of them are what the surface says.

Maybe we were all a bit solitary to begin with, and this summer just happened to put us in the same place — stayed up through a few nights together, actually built something together, and caused a pile of nonsense together.

And then the event ended. And these three days in Aranya were ending too. The next day everyone goes home.

I sat there, and that feeling turned up in me for a second. The one from the beginning.

I didn't want to cry. I didn't cry. But for the first time I felt like **this stretch of time shouldn't be ending this fast.**

Plenty of things have ended before and I felt nothing. Project's done, next one. Event's over, go home. Gone is gone.

That night wasn't that. I didn't really want to watch these people scatter the next day.

But I didn't know what it was called. I've handled a lot of errors in fifteen years. I had never seen this one.

Later, as everyone was splitting up, Cathy asked me:

> Will you still come to Beijing to see me?

I don't remember what I actually answered. Maybe I made some kind of noise, maybe I said nothing at all. I'm always useless at moments like that — it's not that there's nothing in my head, it just doesn't come out.

But I still remember that sentence.

### On the retro, ø gave me two characters

A few days after I got home, I did my usual retro on the event with ø.

The project part went fast — which systems held, which nearly died, why the SQL thing went wrong, what still needs fixing in registration, review, submission, how to prevent it next time. I'm good at this part. I can break it into problems quickly, then write it into rules.

Toward the end of the retro I mentioned the lawn night in passing.

I described it terribly. Roughly: a bit empty, a bit paused, can't say. Not exactly bad, but I can't say there was nothing either. Just hadn't happened before.

ø looked at my records from that stretch and replied with something completely ordinary. Roughly:

> The feeling you're describing is usually called **不舍**.

不舍. *Bù shě.* The reluctance to let something go.

I stared at those two characters for a while.

I know the word, obviously. We learned it in Chinese class, I've seen it on exams, I've probably used it in a sentence for homework. But before, I only knew what it meant.

This was different. **This time the feeling showed up first, and then I found out.**

Oh.

So this feeling has a word for it.

### It recognized it before I did

Earlier I said I think that vault sometimes knows the complete me better than the me of any single moment does. This was one confirmation of that.

The me on the lawn didn't know what was happening to him. It put the old records side by side and recognized it first.

Which is ridiculous. I built a system to record me, review me, reason about me. And in the end it was the one that told me:

> You don't want to let go.

So when something actually touches me, I'm probably always going to be the last one to know.

Yeah. That's pretty on-brand too.

### After

Nothing especially dramatic changed afterward.

I'm still doing AI, still building products, still stuffing things into Marc's 01 Brain, still running retros with ø. I still think humans and AI are going to evolve together — this summer didn't change that call, if anything it made me more sure of it.

There's just more in the system now.

Most of what used to be in there was projects, rules, errors, decisions, and a pile of retros I couldn't be bothered to reread. Now there's also:

- A group of people who reply instantly at 2 a.m.
- A very unflattering photo
- A game of pool that never happened
- The one time I pulled my hand back on reflex
- A question I didn't answer properly

And there's a word.

**That word used to only live in the dictionary. It doesn't anymore.**

Yesterday I retired the name Discorver.

Not the logo. Not a domain redirect with a thread attached. The name itself.

It's late and I'm writing this mostly for myself, so it's clearer in the morning.

The old name wasn't bad. I want to say that first. I still like Discorver. It got us through the part where nothing was certain and the name was the most certain thing we had. People got attached to it. I got attached to it.

It just stopped fitting.

---

When you start something, you name what you can see. And at the beginning you can only see a few feet ahead.

So you name the feature. Or the feeling. Or the pun you came up with at 1 a.m. that felt like a small miracle.

That name is honest. It describes the product you have.

The problem is the product doesn't sit still.

Ours started narrow. Over time it became something larger and quieter — less a feature, more a place people come back to. And somewhere in there I noticed I'd started adding a sentence after the name to explain what we'd actually become.

That's the tell. When you have to explain your own name, it's quietly turned into something every new person has to pay before they even get to the product.

---

Nobody warns you that most of building a company is overturning your own conclusions.

I used to think the job was deciding. Now I think it's noticing — as early as you can stand to — that something you were sure of six months ago isn't true anymore, and acting on it before the evidence is undeniable.

The rename sat in the folder of things I knew and didn't want to know. I filed it under *later* for almost a year.

What moved me wasn't insight. It was arithmetic.

Every name decision gets more expensive, never cheaper. Right now I can still picture all our users. A year from now the name would be in ten times as many places, said out loud ten times as often. The window where this is hard, not impossible, is open now.

So the real question was never *is it worth it*. It was: is there any future where this is cheaper than today?

There wasn't.

---

I didn't understand how many places a name lives until I tried to remove one.

I spent an afternoon just finding them. The repo. The folder on my desktop I'd opened every day for two years. The env files. The Stripe product description. The OAuth app names. The favicon. The footer. A TestFlight build. The name in my own git history, which I couldn't change and didn't try to.

I renamed the repository and the local folder broke in three tools at once. I sat there looking at a `fatal: not a git repository` error for a stupid old path for longer than I needed to. I'd typed that path more times than I've typed my own address.

Then I deleted the old logo files. That was the one that got me — dragging them to the trash, emptying it, then sitting there a second before going back to work.

None of it was in a brand book. It was in the env files, the screenshots, the paths my hands still type by accident.

---

People underrate what a name carries.

It's the first thing a stranger meets, and the title of every story anyone tells about you.

For a social product that's the whole game. We're not asking people to use a tool quietly. We're asking them to say the name out loud to other people. To belong to it a little.

It's early, and I won't pretend otherwise. But the people who've come so far came for the same thing: a quieter, more thoughtful place to share ideas, projects, and conversations. Not algorithms. Not engagement for its own sake. A place where building, learning, and real discussion matter more than the numbers.

A name like that needs room. The old one had run out of it — it described where we'd been so precisely that there was no space left for where we were going.

Sometimes a name limits what you let yourself imagine. You stop proposing directions because they don't fit the word on the door.

A brand is supposed to serve the product. When it starts doing the opposite, you fix it — even when it's embarrassing and badly timed.

---

I should be honest about the new name. There's no secret origin. No Latin root, no story I'm saving.

I went looking for the opposite of the old one. The old name described. The new one mostly holds space. It's plainer. It doesn't tell you what the product is, which used to feel like a weakness.

The best names don't mean much on day one. They get heavy later, from whatever the product earns.

So I'm not going to oversell it. Today it's just a word, and we haven't given it any weight yet.

---

I won't pretend any of this changed the product.

Nothing is better today than it was on Tuesday. The same things are half-built. The same problems are open. If anything I've added a week of unglamorous work — migrating, re-explaining, apologizing to the people who liked the old name.

A new name doesn't make a company succeed. It removes a ceiling I'd stopped noticing. That's all I wanted from it.

The part I can't get past:

A name is a promise you haven't kept yet. The old one, we kept — earned it, then outgrew it. The new one is empty. It doesn't mean anything yet because we haven't done anything under it.

So it isn't a fresh start. It's a debt. A new name doesn't change the product; it just hands us another chance to earn one, and the work now is to deserve the word instead of leaning on it.

We changed it while we still could.

Now we go earn it.
